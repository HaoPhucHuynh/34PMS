The data are $f_i = f(x_i)$ for $i = 0, 1, \dots, n$. I approximate them with the polynomial
\[
p_m(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k x^k,
\]
and I look for the coefficients $c_0, c_1, \dots, c_m$ that make
\[
\chi^2 = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \left[p_m(x_i) - f_i\right]^2
\]
as small as possible.

\medskip
\noindent 1) Analytical formulas for the coefficients

\noindent $\chi^2$ is a function of the $m+1$ coefficients. At the minimum, all its partial derivatives are zero:
\[
\frac{\partial \chi^2}{\partial c_l} = 0, \qquad l = 0, 1, \dots, m.
\]
First I need the derivative of $p_m(x_i) = c_0 + c_1 x_i + \dots + c_m x_i^m$. Only the term $c_l x_i^l$ depends on $c_l$, so
\[
\frac{\partial p_m(x_i)}{\partial c_l} = x_i^l.
\]
With the chain rule,
\[
\frac{\partial \chi^2}{\partial c_l} = \sum_{i=0}^{n} 2\left[p_m(x_i) - f_i\right] x_i^l = 0.
\]
Next I divide by 2 and put in $p_m(x_i) = \sum_k c_k x_i^k$:
\[
\sum_{i=0}^{n} \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k\, x_i^{k}\, x_i^{l} = \sum_{i=0}^{n} f_i\, x_i^l.
\]
Both sums are finite, so I can swap them. $c_k$ does not depend on $i$, so it comes out of the sum over $i$:
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{m} \left(\sum_{i=0}^{n} x_i^{l+k}\right) c_k = \sum_{i=0}^{n} f_i\, x_i^l, \qquad l = 0, 1, \dots, m.
\]
These sums only depend on the data, so I give them names:
\[
s_j = \sum_{i=0}^{n} x_i^{j}, \quad j = 0, 1, \dots, 2m, \qquad\qquad
t_l = \sum_{i=0}^{n} f_i\, x_i^{l}, \quad l = 0, 1, \dots, m.
\]
$l + k$ goes up to $2m$, so I need $s_j$ up to $j = 2m$. Note that $s_0 = n+1$, because $x_i^0 = 1$.
Then the equations are
\[
\sum_{k=0}^{m} s_{l+k}\, c_k = t_l, \qquad l = 0, 1, \dots, m.
\]
These are $m+1$ linear equations for the $m+1$ unknowns $c_k$. In matrix form, $S c = t$:
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
s_0 & s_1 & \cdots & s_m \\
s_1 & s_2 & \cdots & s_{m+1} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
s_m & s_{m+1} & \cdots & s_{2m}
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} c_0 \\ c_1 \\ \vdots \\ c_m \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} t_0 \\ t_1 \\ \vdots \\ t_m \end{pmatrix},
\]
so $S_{lk} = s_{l+k}$. $S$ is symmetric, because $s_{l+k} = s_{k+l}$.

If $\det S \neq 0$, Cramer's rule gives all the coefficients:
\[
c_k = \frac{\det S^{(k)}}{\det S}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, m,
\]
where $S^{(k)}$ is $S$ with column $k$ replaced by $t$:
\[
S^{(k)} =
\begin{pmatrix}
s_0 & \cdots & s_{k-1} & t_0 & s_{k+1} & \cdots & s_m \\
s_1 & \cdots & s_{k} & t_1 & s_{k+2} & \cdots & s_{m+1} \\
\vdots & & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\
s_m & \cdots & s_{m+k-1} & t_m & s_{m+k+1} & \cdots & s_{2m}
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
This is the same as $c = S^{-1} t$.

Cramer's rule needs $\det S \neq 0$. I show that this is true if at least $m+1$ of the $x_i$ are different (so $n \geq m$).
For any vector $v = (v_0, v_1, \dots, v_m)$, I use $s_{l+k} = \sum_i x_i^l x_i^k$ and write $q(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} v_k x^k$:
\[
v^T S v = \sum_{l=0}^{m} \sum_{k=0}^{m} v_l\, s_{l+k}\, v_k
= \sum_{i=0}^{n} \left(\sum_{l=0}^{m} v_l x_i^l\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{m} v_k x_i^k\right)
= \sum_{i=0}^{n} q(x_i)^2 \geq 0.
\]
If $v \neq 0$, $q$ is not the zero polynomial and its degree is at most $m$, so it has at most $m$ roots.
Then $q(x_i) \neq 0$ for at least one of the $m+1$ different $x_i$, so $v^T S v > 0$. Therefore, $S$ is positive definite.
If $\det S = 0$, there is a $v \neq 0$ with $S v = 0$, so $v^T S v = 0$. That is not possible, so $\det S \neq 0$ and there is exactly one solution.

The solution is also a minimum of $\chi^2$, not a maximum or a saddle point. I change the coefficients from the solution $c_k$ to $c_k + \delta_k$.
Then $p_m(x_i)$ changes by $q(x_i) = \sum_k \delta_k x_i^k$, and with $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\chi^2(c + \delta) &= \sum_{i=0}^{n} \left[p_m(x_i) - f_i + q(x_i)\right]^2 \\
&= \sum_{i=0}^{n} \left[p_m(x_i) - f_i\right]^2 + \sum_{l=0}^{m} \delta_l \left(\sum_{i=0}^{n} 2\left[p_m(x_i) - f_i\right] x_i^l\right) + \sum_{i=0}^{n} q(x_i)^2 \\
&= \chi^2(c) + \sum_{l=0}^{m} \delta_l\, \frac{\partial \chi^2}{\partial c_l} + \delta^T S\, \delta.
\end{aligned}
\]
The middle term is zero, because $\partial \chi^2 / \partial c_l = 0$ at the solution. The last term is $\delta^T S \delta > 0$ for $\delta \neq 0$.
Therefore, $\chi^2(c + \delta) > \chi^2(c)$ for every change $\delta$, so the solution is the minimum of $\chi^2$.

Check with $m = 1$, a straight line. Then
\[
S = \begin{pmatrix} n+1 & s_1 \\ s_1 & s_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det S = (n+1)s_2 - s_1^2,
\]
and Cramer's rule gives
\[
c_0 = \frac{1}{\det S}\det\begin{pmatrix} t_0 & s_1 \\ t_1 & s_2 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{s_2 t_0 - s_1 t_1}{(n+1)s_2 - s_1^2},
\]
\[
c_1 = \frac{1}{\det S}\det\begin{pmatrix} n+1 & t_0 \\ s_1 & t_1 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{(n+1)t_1 - s_1 t_0}{(n+1)s_2 - s_1^2}.
\]
This is the linear fit in section 2.2 of the book (Eqs.\ 2.29 and 2.30, with the top and bottom multiplied by $-1$).

\medskip
\noindent 2) Main steps of the numerical algorithm

\noindent Numerically I do not use Cramer's rule. One determinant costs about as much as one Gaussian elimination, so Cramer's rule needs $m+2$ eliminations, while solving $S c = t$ directly needs only one.

Step 1: Input the data $x_i$, $f_i$ for $i = 0, 1, \dots, n$ and the order $m$. At least $m+1$ of the $x_i$ must be different, so that $\det S \neq 0$ (see 1)).

Step 2: Compute the sums $s_j$ for $j = 0, 1, \dots, 2m$ and $t_l$ for $l = 0, 1, \dots, m$, in one loop over the data points.
For each point I get the powers by multiplying with $x_i$ every time, $x_i^{j+1} = x_i^j \cdot x_i$, so I do not need a power function.

Step 3: Fill the $(m+1) \times (m+1)$ matrix $S_{lk} = s_{l+k}$ and the vector $t$.

Step 4: Solve $S c = t$ with Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting (section 5.3 in the book).
For each column $j$, I swap row $j$ with the row (from $j$ down) that has the largest $|S_{rj}|$, and I swap the same two elements of $t$. This way I never divide by a zero or very small pivot.
Then I subtract $S_{rj}/S_{jj}$ times row $j$ from every row $r$ below it (also in $t$), so the elements below the diagonal become zero.
Now $S$ is upper triangular, and I find the coefficients by back substitution, from $c_m$ up to $c_0$:
\[
c_l = \frac{1}{S_{ll}}\left(t_l - \sum_{k=l+1}^{m} S_{lk}\, c_k\right),
\]
with $S$ and $t$ after the elimination.

Step 5: Output $c_0, c_1, \dots, c_m$.

\newpage
\noindent 3) Subprogram

\noindent Schematically, the subprogram for steps 1 to 5 and the Gaussian elimination it calls are:

\begin{verbatim}
subprogram leastSquares(x[0..n], f[0..n], m) returns c[0..m]
    #Step 2: the sums s_j and t_l
    s[0..2m] = 0
    t[0..m] = 0
    for i = 0 to n
        xp = 1                        #xp = x_i^j, starts with x_i^0 = 1
        for j = 0 to 2m
            s[j] = s[j] + xp
            if j <= m: t[j] = t[j] + f[i]*xp
            xp = xp*x[i]              #next power of x_i
    #Step 3: fill S
    for l = 0 to m
        for k = 0 to m
            S[l][k] = s[l+k]
    #Step 4: solve S c = t
    c = gauss(S, t)
    #Step 5
    return c

subprogram gauss(A[0..N-1][0..N-1], b[0..N-1]) returns x[0..N-1]
    #elimination with partial pivoting
    for j = 0 to N-2
        piv = the row r >= j with the largest |A[r][j]|
        swap rows j and piv of A, and b[j] and b[piv]
        for r = j+1 to N-1
            fac = A[r][j]/A[j][j]
            for k = j to N-1
                A[r][k] = A[r][k] - fac*A[j][k]
            b[r] = b[r] - fac*b[j]
    #back substitution
    for r = N-1 down to 0
        x[r] = (b[r] - sum of A[r][k]*x[k] for k = r+1 to N-1)/A[r][r]
    return x
\end{verbatim}
Here $N = m+1$.
